If you read nothing else

The 30-second version

  1. CAM means health practices outside the mainstream biomedical system. Complementary means used alongside medicine. Alternative means used instead of it. The person decides which.
  2. Rongoā Māori is traditional Māori healing: plant remedies (rongoā rākau), massage (mirimiri), prayer (karakia) and water (wai). It is holistic and includes wairua. Responsibility sits with the tohunga/practitioner.
  3. A nurse always asks about CAM and rongoā in the medication history, because they can interact with prescribed medicines.
  4. A nurse doesn't administer a complementary medicine unless an authorised prescriber has prescribed it and it is a registered medicine. Otherwise the nurse can teach the person to self-administer.
  5. Socioeconomic factors decide whether someone can actually get, afford, understand and keep taking a medicine. Examples are cost, transport, income, health literacy and language. Nurses adjust their care for these (that's equity) and advocate.

Exam-shaped

Words to define

Definitions follow the NZNO position statement (2011) and the NZNO medicines guidelines (2024).

Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM)
A broad set of health care practices that are not part of the dominant (biomedical) health system. Some countries use the term interchangeably with traditional medicine.
e.g. herbal remedies, acupuncture, chiropractic, massage, reiki.
Complementary therapy
A therapy used alongside western biomedical treatment, supplementing it or meeting more of the person's needs. It is the term nursing uses most.
e.g. therapeutic massage for relaxation while on prescribed analgesia.
Alternative therapy
A therapy outside the biomedical paradigm, chosen as an alternative to biomedical care.
e.g. using naturopathy instead of a prescribed medicine.
Integrative / integrated medicine
Combining evidence-based complementary therapies with orthodox biomedical diagnosis and treatment. The lecture's point is that large gaps in the evidence remain.
e.g. a patient on standard diabetes care who also uses a rongoā tonic, with both shared openly with the health team.
Traditional medicine
Knowledge, skills and practices based on the theories, beliefs and experiences of indigenous cultures. It is used to maintain health and to prevent, diagnose or treat illness (WHO).
e.g. rongoā Māori, Pacific traditional healing, Ayurveda.
Rongoā Māori
Traditional Māori healing. It is a holistic system that includes wairuatanga (spirituality) alongside physical, mental and social health. It is locally specific, so practice varies by iwi, hapū and whānau.
e.g. kawakawa balm (rongoā rākau), mirimiri, karakia.
Natural health products (NHPs)
As defined in the Therapeutic Products Act 2023: vitamin and mineral supplements, herbal remedies, animal extracts, probiotics, enzymes and essential fatty acids.
e.g. fish oil capsules, a vitamin D supplement.
Placebo effect
A positive outcome that comes from believing a beneficial treatment was received. NZNO notes that placebo, and the relationship with the practitioner, are part of why people report benefit from CAM.
e.g. feeling better after an aromatherapy session that trials show has no measurable effect.
Health literacy
“The capacity to obtain, process and understand basic health information and services in order to make informed and appropriate health decisions” (Ministry of Health, 2015).
e.g. a patient who can't read the label takes the wrong dose, so the nurse teaches using plain language and teach-back.
The module's PowerPoint

Rongoā Māori

From the Rongoā lecture (“Rongoā: the gifts of the god of peace, Rongomātāne”), the same deck as the 15 Sep guest lecture.

What it involves

  • Rongoā rākau: remedies from native plants (leaves, bark, berries, moss)
  • Mirimiri / romiromi: body work in three parts (chiropractic, deep tissue, psychosomatic)
  • Karakia and ritenga: prayer and ritual. A strong spiritual element goes into preparing the remedies
  • Wai: healing with water

Why it matters in Aotearoa

  • Māori life expectancy is 7.1 years shorter than non-Māori, despite Te Tiriti guaranteeing equal rights
  • Health is a taonga, and Māori kept tino rangatiratanga over taonga
  • The Waitangi Tribunal (Wai 262) says rongoā could significantly improve Māori health and should get Crown support

Two models of health (the deck's opening contrast)

Biomedical medicine is reductionist: it treats the part that's broken. Wholistic medicine, which includes rongoā, treats the whole person (body, mind, wairua, whānau and environment). Integrated medicine tries to bring the two together.

The three policies on the slides

  1. NZNO Guidelines for Nurses on the Administration of Medicines (2024) §7.7: the tohunga/practitioner is responsible for rongoā. When whānau ask a nurse for advice, discuss it with everyone involved, including the tohunga.
  2. NZNO position statement: Rongoā Māori and Complementary Therapies in Nursing Practice (2011): the nurse's role, covered in the “Nurse's role” section below.
  3. Tikanga ā Rongoā (Ministry of Health, 2014): a voluntary standard for rongoā providers, setting a benchmark for safe, quality rongoā care.

Other tools from the lecture

  • Te Maramataka: the Māori lunar calendar, used to predict mood and activity levels
  • Te Whare Tapa Whā: the four-walls model of hauora
  • Matakite: “to see in front of the face”, a traditional way of knowing used in diagnosis
  • Narrative as therapy: karakia, kōrero, pūrākau and waiata (prayer, talk, stories and song)

NZNO position statement

Complementary and alternative medicine

CAM use is common and rising. In 2008 the Ministry of Health found about 18% of NZ adults had used a complementary therapy in the past year. WHO estimates 70–80% of people in developed countries use one at some point, and a Christchurch study found 70% of children at GP and outpatient clinics had used one.

The five CAM groups (MACCAH, 2004)

GroupExamples
1 · Alternative medical systemsAyurveda, traditional Chinese medicine (acupuncture), Pacific traditional healing, homoeopathy, naturopathy
2 · Mind–body–spiritHypnotherapy, spiritual healing, Bach flower, counselling and psychotherapies
3 · Biological-basedHerbal medicine, Chinese herbal medicine, dietary approaches
4 · Manipulative and body-basedChiropractic, osteopathy, therapeutic massage, Alexander technique, Bowen
5 · Energy therapiesTai chi, qi gong, yoga, reiki, shiatsu

Rongoā Māori is not in the MACCAH groups. It is its own system.

Does it work? Some does, some doesn't

Evidence of benefit

  • Cranberry for 12 months reduces recurrent UTIs in women
  • St John's wort for mild to moderate depression
  • Functional relaxation for irritable bowel syndrome
  • Honey eases cough frequency and severity in upper respiratory infections (a BMJ review in the course's “for interest” readings)

No benefit found

  • The herbal formula CUF2 for asthma in children
  • Aromatherapy massage in cancer care, which made no difference to mood, quality of life or symptoms

The catch with “evidence”: randomised controlled trials suit biomedical drugs but can undersell traditional healing. NZNO says evidence should come from many sources, and the most important source is the benefit the person reports. Safety still comes first.

Interactions: the reason it's a pharmacology topic

A review in the course readings found 78 herbs, foods and supplements that interact with warfarin. About 58% increased its effect, which means a higher bleeding risk. About 30% reduced it. Examples include cranberry, chamomile, green tea, ginkgo, ginger, St John's wort and cannabis. Most of that evidence is from case reports, so it's weak. The rule that follows: people should ask a health professional before starting any new herb or supplement, and nurses should ask about them.

The most examinable part

The nurse's role

1 · Always ask

Asking about complementary therapies is part of every nursing assessment, because they can interact with medicines. The medication chart should have a section for complementary, alternative, Rongoā or self-prescribed medicines (NZNO guidelines).

2 · The five responsibilities (NZNO)

  • Competence: the RN is accountable under the HPCA Act 2003 and must stay within their scope of practice
  • Information: the person gets accurate, timely, evidence-based information so they can make an informed decision
  • Consent: required before any intervention, CAM included
  • Authority: only use a therapy you are trained and competent in, and that your workplace authorises
  • Outcomes and interactions: know them, and document every intervention

3 · When someone asks for advice

Hold a discussion with everyone involved: client, pharmacist, prescriber, and for rongoā, the tohunga. Consider four things:

  1. Is there any evidence-based information on it?
  2. Is it appropriate for the condition?
  3. What are the possible side effects?
  4. Could it interact with prescribed medicines?

For rongoā, add a fifth: the practitioner's knowledge and experience.

4 · Administering

NZNO advises nurses not to administer complementary medicines unless they are prescribed by an authorised prescriber and are a registered medicine. Don't give unregistered ones. Sometimes the right move is to teach the person to self-administer.

5 · Ethics and cultural safety

  • It is the person's choice whether to use CAM or rongoā as a complement or an alternative (autonomy)
  • The Code of Ethics principles apply: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, confidentiality, veracity and fidelity
  • Nurses hold power, so endorsing a particular therapy carries risk for the client and the nurse
  • Ethical conflict arises when the nurse's beliefs differ from the person's, or when a therapy could cause harm
  • For most nurses, advocating for or referring to rongoā is the culturally safe way to include it (NZNO)
  • Te Tiriti, Whakamarumarutia (active protection): acknowledge and respect rongoā alongside prescribed medication, and advocate for the wider team to do the same
From Joan's own decks

Socioeconomic factors in pharmacology

From Pharmacology Concepts (1), Medication Administration & Patient Education (2), and Medication in the Elderly & Polypharmacy.

A medicine only works if the person can get it, afford it, understand it and keep taking it. Socioeconomic factors affect every one of those steps, and they drive non-adherence. (“Adherence” has replaced the older word “compliance”.)

FactorHow it affects medicines
Cost and incomePrescription charges and unfunded medicines cost money. Older people often have reduced income. With polypharmacy, cost and pill burden add up
Funding (PHARMAC)PHARMAC decides which medicines are funded within a fixed budget. Generic prescribing lets the pharmacist dispense the subsidised brand, which is cheaper for the person and the funder
Access“Can they drive to the pharmacy? Is there a cost?” Transport, distance and getting to a prescriber all matter
Health literacyLow health literacy leads to wrong doses, missed doses and poor self-management
Language and cultureDoes the person need an interpreter? Do their beliefs or worldview shape how they use medicines? People with language barriers are more prone to prescribing errors
DisabilitySensory or cognitive impairment, and difficulty with access, storage or administration
InequityColonisation, barriers to access and the burden of long-term conditions drive health inequities, most clearly for Māori

What the nurse does about it

  • Equity (Te Tiriti, Mana Taurite) means unequal distribution for an equal outcome. Allow more time for people who need it, and adjust teaching to their life: cost, transport, literacy and pharmacy access
  • Health literacy ABC: Ask what the person already knows, Build the knowledge they need in plain language, Check you were clear with teach-back. Teach-back is not a test of the patient
  • Ask about cost and access openly, and advocate through the prescriber, pharmacist or social worker
  • Tino rangatiratanga (self-determination): ask “How do you feel about starting this medication?” and fit it to the person's routines and beliefs

NZ context, not on Joan's slides (checked online 14 Oct 2026): a prescription item costs $5. It is free for children 13 and under, people 65 and over, and Community Services Card holders. After 20 paid items a year, a family gets a Prescription Subsidy Card, and it resets on 1 February.

Self-check

Say your answer out loud, then tap to compare. The questions and model answers are mine, written from the sources on this page. They are not Joan's.

Define complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) and give an example.Show

CAM is a broad set of health care practices outside the dominant biomedical health system. Complementary therapies are used alongside medical treatment, and alternative therapies are used instead of it.

Example
Using therapeutic massage alongside prescribed pain relief (complementary).

What is Rongoā Māori? Give an example.Show

Rongoā Māori is traditional Māori healing, a holistic system that includes wairua alongside physical, mental and social health. It covers rongoā rākau (plant remedies), mirimiri (massage), karakia (prayer) and wai (water). The tohunga/practitioner is responsible for it.

Example
Kawakawa balm applied to the skin.

A patient asks you about a herbal remedy. What do you consider?Show

Discuss it with the client, pharmacist and prescriber. Consider whether there is evidence-based information on it, whether it suits the condition, its side effects, and possible interactions with prescribed medicines. For rongoā, include the tohunga and consider their knowledge and experience. Document the discussion.

A patient brings in their own herbal supplement and asks you to give it with their meds. Do you?Show

No, not unless an authorised prescriber has prescribed it and it is a registered medicine (NZNO). Don't administer unregistered complementary medicines. You can report it to the prescriber and pharmacist to check for interactions, and where appropriate teach the person to self-administer. Document it on the chart.

What is integrative medicine? Give an example.Show

Integrative medicine combines evidence-based complementary therapies with orthodox biomedical diagnosis and treatment.

Example
A person with type 2 diabetes using a rongoā tonic alongside their prescribed treatment, with the health team aware and monitoring blood glucose.

Why must a nurse ask about CAM in the medication history?Show

CAM can interact with prescribed medicines and make them stronger or weaker, which risks harm or treatment failure.

Example
Many herbs, including cranberry and ginkgo, increase the effect of warfarin, which raises bleeding risk.

Define health literacy and give an example of how it affects medication use.Show

Health literacy is the capacity to obtain, process and understand basic health information and services in order to make informed and appropriate health decisions (MoH, 2015).

Example
A patient who misreads “twice daily” takes double doses. The nurse uses plain language and teach-back to check understanding.

Name two socioeconomic factors that affect medication adherence, with an example.Show

Cost or income: a person on a low income skips filling a prescription because of the charge. Access: a person with no transport can't get to the pharmacy. Both lead to non-adherence. The nurse adjusts the plan and advocates, which is equity in practice.
