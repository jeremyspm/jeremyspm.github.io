The marking sheet, in order

The vital signs routine

Before you go in

  1. Check the chart: obs are due, and it's the right patient.
  2. Say your plan and order to the RN: temp, pulse, resps, BP.
  3. Equipment, checked it works. The right cuff size (he needs the larger one).
  4. Hand hygiene.

At the bedside

  1. Greet him, and introduce yourself AND your preceptor.
  2. ID: ask his full name and DOB, then check the wristband: name, DOB AND NHI against the chart.
  3. Ask what he'd like to be called (culturally safe practice).
  4. Explain: “I'll touch your ear, your wrist and your arm, and the cuff gets pumped up twice.”
  5. Get consent.
  6. Make the space safe: curtain for privacy, brakes on, area clear, him sitting with feet flat, kit within reach.

The obs

  1. Temp (tympanic): new probe cover, pull the ear up and back, read it, eject the cover. Chart it straight away (an X), never on a paper towel.
  2. Pulse (radial): two or three fingers, not your thumb. 30 s × 2. Irregular = count a full minute.
  3. Resps: keep your fingers on his wrist and don't announce it. 30 s × 2. Note depth and effort.
  4. Chart: HR as an X, RR as the number in its box.
  5. BP, two-step (below). Chart it as an arrowhead pointing up on the systolic, one pointing down on the diastolic, joined by a dotted line.

Report and leave him safe

  1. Tell him: “I'm just going to talk to my preceptor about your results. Is that okay?”
  2. Report every value WITH its normal range, plus the date and 24-hour time, and that you counted for 30 s × 2.
  3. The RN asks what you'd do if one is abnormal. Say: “Recheck it, document it, and report it to the RN straight away.”
  4. Close the loop (“we'll be back in half an hour”). Call bell, water, comfortable. Thank him.
  5. Clean the equipment and put it back. Hand hygiene. Check the chart is complete.
The step people get pulled up on

Two-step blood pressure

  1. Position: bare arm, supported at heart level, palm up. Him sitting, feet flat, not talking.
  2. Check the cuff size with its range lines. Too small reads falsely HIGH.
  3. Find the brachial artery (inside of the elbow crease). Cuff fully deflated, artery marker on the artery, 2–3 cm above the crease, two fingers fit under it.
  4. STEP 1: feel the radial pulse, pump until it disappears, then 30 more. No stethoscope yet.
  5. Let it down slowly. Where the pulse comes back = the estimated systolic (e.g. 110).
  6. Deflate fully and wait 30 seconds.
  7. STEP 2: stethoscope ON the brachial BEFORE you pump. Earpieces pointing forward. Tap to check it's open. Rest it lightly below the cuff, not tucked under.
  8. Pump to estimate + 30 (110 → 140).
  9. Release at 2–3 mmHg per second. First clear tap = systolic. Sound disappears = diastolic.
  10. Tell him in plain words: “110 over 60. That's normal, on the low side. Do you know what yours usually is?”

Med admin

Marked on the rubric

The two drugs

Have three things ready for whichever drug you get: the generic name, why HE is on it, and its common side effects.

Every drug: say anaphylaxis

Assessors treat anaphylaxis as a critical point for every medicine, even when it isn't a common side effect. Leaving it out can fail the assessment. When you list side effects, end with: “and, rarely, an allergic reaction or anaphylaxis: rash, swelling, trouble breathing.” Tell him in plain words too: “If you get a rash, swelling, or trouble breathing, ring your bell straight away.”

Paracetamol

Pack may say
Pacimol / Panadol / Pamol
Generic + class
Paracetamol, an analgesic (it also lowers fever)
Order
1 g PO qid = two 500 mg tabs
Why he's on it
His recurrent backache
Side effects
None common at the right dose. All very rare: rash or allergy, bronchospasm, low platelets, liver problems. The big risk is liver damage if he has too much.
Check first
When was his last dose? At least 4 h apart, max 4 g in 24 h. 1 g qid is already 4 g, so no other paracetamol at all.
Critical: anaphylaxis
Rare, but say it: rash, swelling of the face or throat, trouble breathing. Stop, call for help, and follow the emergency protocol.
Must say
Ask about Panadol or cold-and-flu medicines from home (Codral, Lemsip, Coldrex, Maxigesic, Nuromol all contain paracetamol).

Metoprolol

Pack may say
Betaloc CR / Myloc CR
Generic + class
Metoprolol succinate CR, a beta blocker
Order
47.5 mg PO daily (mane) = one CR tab
Why he's on it
His high BP (higher than usual lately) and his heart failure
Side effects
Very common: tiredness. Common: slow pulse, dizzy on standing, cold hands and feet, palpitations, dizziness, headache, nausea, tummy pain, diarrhoea, constipation, breathless when active.
Check first
Pulse and BP. If either is low, withhold it and tell the RN.
Critical: anaphylaxis
Say it, even though it's rare. Watch for rash, swelling of the face or throat, wheeze, trouble breathing. Beta blockers can make an anaphylactic reaction more severe and harder to treat with adrenaline (data sheet warning). Stop, call for help, follow the emergency protocol.
Must say
Don't crush it (CR; it can be halved). Don't stop it suddenly, because his heart failure can get worse. Stand up slowly and ring the bell first (falls risk; he's 79).
Beyond the common list

Know everything about your drug

The rubric asks for the generic name, the indication and the common side effects. Assessors can go further, so here's the rest, with the same 14 headings for both drugs. Unless tagged Course, it's from the Medsafe data sheets and Healthify, not from your lectures.

Paracetamol

How it works
An analgesic and antipyretic. It acts mainly in the brain (it blocks prostaglandin production there), so it eases pain and lowers fever but does little for inflammation.
Onset and half-life
Works in about 30–60 min and lasts 4–6 h. Half-life 1–3 h; most of a dose is out in the urine within 24 h. Course
ADME
Absorbed rapidly (food can slow it). Distributed widely, little protein-bound. Metabolised in the liver: a small amount becomes a toxic metabolite that builds up in overdose. Excreted in the urine. Course
Form and how to take it
500 mg tablets: two tablets for 1 g. Swallow with a glass of water, food or not. A liquid exists if he can't swallow tablets.
Withhold and tell the RN if
The last dose was under 4 h ago, the 24-h total would go over 4 g, or he's taken his own paracetamol. Any sign of allergy, or yellow skin or eyes (liver).
Don't give if
He's allergic to paracetamol, he's already had his max for 24 h, or he has severe liver disease.
Take care with
Liver disease, heavy alcohol use, low body weight (under 50 kg) or malnutrition, dehydration, kidney problems, the frail elderly. Check with the RN: the max may need to be lower.
Common side effects
None are common at the right dose. Say exactly that, then go on to the rare ones below.
Rare but serious
Anaphylaxis and allergic reactions (rash, swelling, trouble breathing), bronchospasm in people sensitive to aspirin, serious skin reactions (blistering, peeling rash), low platelets or white cells (unusual bruising, infections), liver damage.
Stopping, or a missed dose
Can be stopped any time, no tapering. Missed dose: take it when he remembers, keeping 4 h between doses and no more than 4 g in 24 h. Never double up.
Overdose
Often no symptoms at first, or just nausea and vomiting. Liver damage shows up 1–3 days later. It's urgent even if he feels fine. The antidote is acetylcysteine.
Monitor
Pain score before, and again 30–60 min after to check it worked. The 24-h total. Temp, if it's for fever. Rash or swelling.
Tell him
What it's for (his back). Don't take any other paracetamol (Panadol, cold-and-flu products): check the labels. Ring your bell if you get a rash, swelling or trouble breathing. Go easy on alcohol. Tell us if the pain isn't easing.
Legal class
A general sales medicine (sold anywhere). Pregnancy category A. Course
Other products that contain it
The “what OTC meds contain this?” question. Cold-and-flu: many Codral, Lemsip and Coldrex products. Combination painkillers: Maxigesic and Nuromol (paracetamol + ibuprofen), paracetamol + codeine (e.g. Panadeine). Panadol Osteo / Apo-Osteo: 665 mg modified-release paracetamol, the same drug. Answer it as safety: he's already on the 4 g max, so any of these takes him over. Bonus: cold-and-flu decongestants raise BP, and the ibuprofen in Maxigesic or Nuromol worsens heart failure.
Why not ibuprofen for his back?
NSAIDs make the body hold fluid and raise BP, so they worsen heart failure and work against his BP medicines. They also add kidney and stomach-bleed risk at 79. Paracetamol doesn't irritate the stomach, which is why it suits older people. Data sheet

Metoprolol succinate CR

How it works
A cardioselective (beta-1) beta blocker, an antagonist: it blocks adrenaline at the heart's beta receptors. The heart beats slower and less forcefully, BP drops, and the heart works less hard. Course
Onset and half-life
Half-life about 3–5 h, but the CR tablet releases it slowly, so one dose covers 24 h. The full BP effect builds over about a week; the heart failure benefit over weeks to months.
ADME
Well absorbed by mouth, but about half is lost on its first pass through the liver. Little protein-bound. Metabolised in the liver (the CYP2D6 enzyme, which is why fluoxetine and paroxetine raise its levels). Excreted in the urine.
Form and how to take it
Controlled release (CR): one 47.5 mg tablet, once a day. Swallow whole with a glass of water, food or not. It can be halved, never crushed or chewed (halves included): the tablet is hundreds of coated beads that release it over about 20 h, and crushing dumps the whole day's dose at once.
Why 47.5 mg?
It's the succinate salt: 47.5 mg succinate = 50 mg metoprolol tartrate. Strengths are 23.75, 47.5, 95 and 190 mg. For heart failure it's started at 23.75 mg and doubled every 2 weeks towards a target of 190 mg daily; for BP the usual start is 47.5 mg. Data sheet
Same drug, other names
Betaloc CR and Myloc CR are both metoprolol succinate CR. Two brands in his drawer = a double dose (the Mr Wei Tan case). Metoprolol tartrate (immediate-release, e.g. Lopresor) is not the same: don't give it in place of succinate CR. Course
Withhold and tell the RN if
His pulse or BP is low. Common ward cut-offs are a pulse under 60 or a systolic under 100, but the chart or your RN sets the actual numbers, so ask. Also if he's dizzy or faint, newly wheezy, or more breathless or swollen than usual.
Don't give if
He's allergic to it or another beta blocker. Asthma or other obstructive lung disease (the NZ data sheet lists it as a contraindication). A very slow pulse, 2nd or 3rd-degree heart block, or sick sinus syndrome (no pacemaker). Very low BP or shock. Unstable or worsening heart failure (e.g. fluid on the lungs). Severe circulation problems in the legs.
Take care with
Any wheeze: it's cardioselective, so less bronchospasm than older beta blockers, but it can still cause it and it blocks salbutamol. Diabetes: it hides hypo signs like a fast heartbeat and tremor (sweating still shows). Liver problems. The elderly (falls). Before surgery, tell the anaesthetist.
Common side effects
Very common: tiredness. Common: slow pulse, dizzy on standing, cold hands and feet, palpitations, dizziness, headache, nausea, tummy pain, diarrhoea, constipation, breathless when active.
Rare but serious
Anaphylaxis, and beta blockers make it more severe and harder to treat with adrenaline. Worsening heart failure (weight gain, swollen ankles, more breathless), heart block, fainting, bronchospasm. Less common: low mood, nightmares, trouble sleeping, confusion, rash, sexual problems.
Stopping, or a missed dose
Never stop it suddenly: rebound high BP, chest pain or a heart attack, and worse heart failure. The body has made extra beta receptors (upregulation), so they over-respond once it's gone. The prescriber tapers it, over at least 2 weeks. Course Missed dose: take it when he remembers, unless the next one is nearly due. Never double up.
Overdose
A very slow pulse, very low BP, heart block, shock, bronchospasm, low blood sugar, collapse. It's an emergency: call for help.
Monitor
Pulse and BP before and after (lying and standing if he's dizzy). Dizziness and falls. Weight, swelling, breathlessness (heart failure). Wheeze. Blood sugar if he's diabetic. Rash or swelling.
Tell him
What it's for (his BP and his heart). Stand up slowly, and ring before getting up. Tiredness often settles after the first weeks. Don't stop it without the doctor. Ring your bell if you get a rash, swelling or trouble breathing. Tell us if you feel faint, more breathless, your ankles swell or you're wheezy. Alcohol can make the dizziness worse.
Legal class
A prescription medicine (prescriber only). Pregnancy category C.
If they ask

Drug interactions

Assessors can ask this because every Medsafe data sheet has an interactions section (4.5). They want to see that you'd stop and check, not a memorised list. If you're stuck, say:

“I'd check the NZF interactions checker or section 4.5 of the data sheet, and talk to the pharmacist or RN before giving it.”

Metoprolol

  • Salbutamol (asthma): works less well, because metoprolol blocks the beta receptors salbutamol needs. Joan, slide 45
  • Other BP drugs (e.g. cilazapril): BP drops further, so more dizziness and falls. Data sheet
  • Verapamil or diltiazem: can slow his heart too much. Data sheet
  • Insulin or diabetes tablets: metoprolol can hide hypo signs, like a fast heartbeat. Data sheet
  • NSAIDs (ibuprofen): can blunt its BP-lowering effect. Data sheet
  • Some antidepressants (fluoxetine, paroxetine): raise metoprolol levels, so a slower pulse. Digoxin: an even slower pulse. Data sheet
  • Cold-and-flu decongestants (pseudoephedrine, phenylephrine): push his BP back up. Alcohol: more dizziness, and it raises metoprolol levels. Data sheet

Paracetamol

  • Other paracetamol products (Panadol, cold-and-flu): a double dose, so a liver risk. Data sheet
  • Warfarin: regular use can raise the INR, so more bleeding risk. Data sheet
  • Heavy alcohol, or enzyme-inducing drugs (carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampicin): more liver risk. Data sheet
  • Metoclopramide speeds up its absorption. Cholestyramine slows it (give paracetamol 1 h before). Data sheet

Joan, slide 45 is from your course. Data sheet is general pharmacology from the Medsafe data sheets (section 4.5), added here and not taught in the course. Don't stress about those.

For the final: metoprolol + salbutamol is the opposing example (an agonist against an antagonist at beta receptors), and warfarin + aspirin is the additive one (more bleeding).

The full run

With your RN, before the bedside

  1. Hand hygiene before you touch the chart or the meds. a10
  2. Read the chart out loud, finger on it: name, NHI, DOB on the sticker, and the prescriber's handwritten name. No name means no drug. a11
  3. Allergies box: “Ticked no, signed and dated by the prescriber. I'll still check with him at the bedside.” a9
  4. Special Care box and supplementary charts: say what's ticked (renal, hepatic, anticoagulation…). a11
  5. Find the order: date, generic name, dose and units, route, frequency, prescriber's signature. a2 a5 d1
  6. Right time: “Prescribed ___. The last dose was ___, so it's due now.” a4
  7. The drug-specific check. Paracetamol: his pain score and the 24-h total. Metoprolol: his pulse and BP, and whether they're OK to give. a11
  8. The RN asks “Tell me about this drug.” Generic name and class, why he's on it, all the common side effects, then anaphylaxis, then a nursing point. k1 k2 k3 a7
  9. Label vs chart (check 1): “The label says ___. It's prescribed ___, so that's ___ tablet(s).” a2 a3
  10. Expiry: read it AND interpret it: “End of July 2027, so it's in date.” a8
  11. Pop it into the cup without touching it. Label vs chart again (check 2), and again as you put the pack away (check 3). Glass of water. a10 a3 a11

At the bedside

  1. Knock, and hand hygiene on the way in. a10
  2. Introduce yourself AND your RN, and say what you've brought. e5
  3. ID: he says his full name and DOB. You check the wristband (name, DOB, NHI) against the chart. a1
  4. Allergies, out loud: “Are you allergic to anything? Ever had a bad reaction to a medicine?” a9
  5. ASK: “Have you heard of ___? What do you know about it?” e1
  6. BUILD, in plain words: what it's for, how to take it, the side effects and what to do, including “if you get a rash, swelling or trouble breathing, ring your bell straight away.” Pause. e2 k1–k3 a7
  7. CHECK: “Can you tell me in your own words…?” Never “Do you understand?” e3
  8. Consent: “Are you happy to take it? Any questions?” a6
  9. Give it: him sitting upright, with water. Stay until it's swallowed. Never leave it on the table. a5 a11
  10. Home tip: “Same time each day, and get a repeat before you run out.” e2
  11. Leave him safe: call bell in reach, “ring before you get up,” thank him. a11 e5
  12. Hand hygiene on the way out. a10

Document and debrief

  1. Sign only AFTER he's taken it: date, 24-h time, dose with units (“47.5 mg”, never “47.5”). Your initials in Giv, your RN's in Chck. d1 d2 d3
  2. The RN asks how you used Ask, Build, Check. Name all three and what you did in each. e4
  3. Thank your RN. e5
Patient education

Ask, Build, Check

ASK
“What have you heard about this medicine?” An open question, not yes or no.
BUILD
What it's for and its side effects, in plain words. Explain any medical term, and pause.
CHECK
“Can you tell me back in your own words what it's for, and what you'd do if…?” Never ask “Do you understand?”

At the end, the RN asks you to describe how you used Ask, Build, Check. Name all three steps.

Treat these as part of the assessment. The marking sheet doesn't list them, but the brief says you must know the “key nursing considerations for safe administration”, and assessors use that line to ask anything safety-related (a classmate got “what other OTC medicines contain this?”). Slip-ups that cost people

  • Signing before he's swallowed it, or leaving it on the table.
  • Touching the tablet, or skipping hand hygiene.
  • “Do you understand?” instead of teach-back. Jargon in Build.
  • A textbook indication instead of his (his BP and heart failure; his backache).
  • Giving it more than 30 min from the charted time without noticing. Course
  • 12-hour time, white-out, or made-up codes. Use 24-h actual time, one line through errors, and only NMC codes (W, R, N, U). N means the prescriber must be told. Course
  • Hiding a mistake. Tell the RN straight away: concealment means disciplinary action. Course
If they ask

What if…

…he has an anaphylactic reaction?
Stop. Stay with him and call for help (emergency bell). Lie him flat with his legs up, or sitting up if he's struggling to breathe. The RN gives IM adrenaline by protocol, plus oxygen. Monitor his obs. Then document it, record the allergy on the chart, and report it to CARM.
…you notice a medication error?
Tell the RN straight away and check on him (obs). Follow the RN's instructions, document it, and fill in an incident report. Be honest.
…he vomits soon after taking it?
Tell the RN. Don't give another dose unless the RN or prescriber says to. Document it.
…he can't swallow tablets?
Paracetamol: ask the RN or pharmacist about a liquid. Metoprolol CR: it can be halved, never crushed. Ask the pharmacist.
…he asks something you don't know?
“That's a good question. I'll check with my RN or the pharmacist and come back to you.” Never guess.
…you suspect a side effect later on?
Assess him, tell the RN, document it. A suspected adverse reaction can be reported to CARM (anyone can report).
…the order is unclear or unsigned?
Don't give it. Tell the RN, who gets the prescriber to clarify it.
Expect these too

Knowledge questions

Not about the drug itself, but fair game under “key nursing considerations”. All from your course decks unless tagged.

Side effect vs adverse effect vs allergy?
A side effect is a known extra effect. An adverse effect is a harmful effect suspected to be caused by the medicine (Medsafe). An allergy is an immune hypersensitivity reaction, and it's a contraindication.
Contraindication vs precaution?
A contraindication: don't use it. A precaution: use it with extra monitoring or a dose change.
Mild allergy vs anaphylaxis?
Mild: rash, hives, itch, lip or eyelid swelling. Anaphylaxis: breathing difficulty, wheeze, throat swelling, low BP, a weak fast pulse, collapse. IM adrenaline and call for help. Joan said to do the Allergy session before this practical.
Is Mr Luke on polypharmacy?
Yes: 3 regular + 2 new = 5 medicines (polypharmacy is usually 5 or more). That means more interactions and a higher falls risk.
How do age and heart failure change things?
Slower liver and kidney clearance, more fat and less body water, more sensitivity to heart and BP drugs. Heart failure cuts blood flow to the liver and kidneys too. The big risks: falls, postural hypotension, delirium.
Who can prescribe? Can you change the dose?
Doctors, nurse practitioners, dentists, optometrists and midwives, plus designated prescribers (pharmacist and RN prescribers). Nurses can't change an order.
Which patient rights apply?
The HDC Code, especially Right 6 (to be fully informed) and Right 7 (informed choice and consent).
What's AI2DET?
Acknowledge, Introduce, Identify, Duration, Explanation, Thank you.
Te Tiriti in medicine education?
Tino rangatiratanga: his choice. Equity: extra time if he needs it. Active protection: respect rongoā. Options: choices of timing and format. Partnership: involve whānau if he wants.
Where do you look a medicine up?
The NZ Formulary and the Medsafe data sheet and consumer information.

Practical Skills Assessment: Vital Signs (722.556)

Met / Not Met. Two attempts. You must pass to pass the course.

Assessment and planning

  • Check patient's chart to identify the need for these observations to be taken, and recorded
  • Collects all appropriate equipment ensuring it is in working order
  • Organises safe environment

Implementation

  • Hand decontamination using hand sanitiser or washing with soap and water
  • Introduces self and confirm how the patient prefers to be addressed
  • Identifies correct patient by: checking patient's name & birth date verbally against chart, and visually on patient's wristband, including NHI, against chart
  • Explains the procedure to the patient that it involves touching their ear and their arm
  • Gain consent from the patient
  • Assess and document vital signs on appropriate chart: temperature (using tympanic thermometer); pulse rate (count for 30 seconds); respiratory rate (count for 30 seconds); blood pressure (using two-step technique)
  • Adheres to infection control practices
  • Communicates effectively with patient
  • Ensures safe environment on leaving patient
  • Cleans and returns equipment
  • Hand decontamination

Evaluation

  • Accurately documents findings directly on vital signs chart
  • Identifies and reports normal/abnormal findings to Registered Nurse (including discussion of normal adult ranges for all vital signs)
  • Systematic manner of completion
  • Demonstrates culturally safe practices
  • Demonstrates therapeutic communication throughout
